● Common Symptoms ●

Find What You're Feeling.

It starts small. A dull ache you can almost ignore. Sensitivity to cold. A crack you can't see. By the time it hurts, the problem is already deeper than the surface. We treat all of it, same day:

  • Urgent

    Severe toothache

    A deep, throbbing ache that keeps you up at night usually means the nerve is involved.

  • Urgent

    Swelling and facial abscess

    Facial swelling means infection is spreading beyond the tooth. This one cannot wait.

  • Urgent

    Dental infection

    Pressure, bad taste, or a pimple on the gum are all signs of an active infection.

  • Urgent

    Knocked-out tooth

    A tooth that has been fully knocked out can often be saved if you get here fast.

  • Urgent

    Loose or displaced tooth

    A tooth pushed out of position or moving in its socket needs to be stabilized quickly.

  • Broken or chipped tooth

    A cracked or broken tooth exposes the inner layers and gets worse with every bite.

  • Lost or loose filling, crown, or bridge

    A filling, crown, or bridge that has come off or come loose leaves the tooth exposed to bacteria and pressure.

  • Dental implant emergency

    A loose, painful, or failing implant needs a surgical eye on it, not a wait list.

  • Extreme sensitivity

    Sharp pain from cold, heat, or air points to exposed dentin or a hidden crack.

  • Wisdom teeth pain

    Pressure at the back of the jaw, swelling, or trouble opening your mouth.

  • Broken denture

● Before You Get Here ●

What to Do Right Now.

The first few minutes can decide whether a tooth is saved and how bad the pain gets. Here's what to do on the way to us.

Guide 01

Severe Toothache

A throbbing ache usually means the nerve is involved. These steps take the edge off until you're seen.

  1. Rinse with warm salt water (half a teaspoon of salt in a glass of water).
  2. Floss gently around the tooth in case food is trapped.
  3. Take an over-the-counter pain reliever as directed on the package.
  4. Hold a cold compress on the outside of your cheek, 20 minutes on, 20 off.
  5. Call (516) 500-7400 to get seen.

Never put aspirin directly on the gum. It burns the tissue.

Guide 02

Knocked-Out Tooth

An adult tooth that comes out can often be saved, but the first hour matters most.

  1. Pick it up by the crown (the white part), never the root.
  2. If it's dirty, rinse it for a few seconds. Don't scrub or dry it.
  3. If you can, push it back into the socket and bite gently on clean gauze.
  4. If not, keep it in a small cup of milk, not water.
  5. Call (516) 500-7400 and come in right away.
Guide 03

Broken or Chipped Tooth

A break exposes the inside of the tooth, so it gets more sensitive and more likely to get infected.

  1. Rinse your mouth gently with warm water.
  2. Save any pieces you find and keep them in milk.
  3. Use a cold compress on your cheek to keep swelling down.
  4. Cover a sharp edge with dental wax or sugar-free gum.
  5. Chew on the other side and call (516) 500-7400.
Guide 04

Swelling or Abscess

Swelling means infection is spreading past the tooth. It won't clear up on its own.

  1. Don't press, pop, or drain a bump on your gum.
  2. Rinse with warm salt water a few times a day.
  3. Use a cold compress on the outside of your face. Skip heat.
  4. Call (516) 500-7400 right away.

Go to the ER or call 911 if you have trouble breathing or swallowing, swelling near your eye or down your neck, or a high fever.

● Emergency Dental FAQ ●

What Patients Ask Us Most.

+What counts as a dental emergency?

Severe tooth pain, a knocked-out or loose tooth, a cracked or broken tooth, swelling in the face or gums, bleeding that won't stop, or a lost filling or crown that's causing pain. If it hurts or something broke, it's worth getting seen.

+Can you treat a dental emergency the same day?

Yes. Same-day emergency care is what this practice is built for. Most patients are seen within about an hour.

+What should I do if my tooth gets knocked out?

Handle it by the crown, not the root. Gently rinse it, and if you can, place it back in the socket or keep it in milk. Then get to us right away. A knocked-out tooth has the best chance of being saved within the first hour.

+Is facial or gum swelling a dental emergency?

Yes. Swelling in the jaw, cheek, or neck can signal a spreading infection or abscess and should be treated quickly. Call us right away.

+Should I go to the ER or a dentist for tooth pain?

For tooth pain, infection, or a broken tooth, an emergency dentist can treat the actual cause. An ER usually can't. If you have difficulty breathing or swallowing, or trauma to the head, call 911 first.

+What will the ER do for tooth pain?

Usually pain medication and sometimes antibiotics, then a referral to a dentist. Most ERs don't have a dentist on staff, so they can't fix the tooth itself. Go to the ER for trouble breathing or swallowing, swelling spreading toward your eye or neck, heavy bleeding, or a jaw or head injury. For everything else, call us at (516) 500-7400.

+Can a dentist pull a broken or decayed tooth?

Yes. When a tooth can't be saved, it can be removed, often during the same emergency visit once imaging shows the roots. Teeth broken below the gumline or impacted wisdom teeth are handled by oral surgeons on site.

+What should I do if my denture breaks?

Don't glue it or try to fix it at home. That can make a repair impossible. Bring the denture and every piece to your visit and we'll check whether it can be repaired or needs replacing. More on denture repair.

+How can I calm my nerves before an emergency dental visit?

Tell us you're nervous when you call. We'll explain each step before we do it, and the area is numbed before any treatment starts. It also helps to bring someone with you, eat a light meal beforehand, and bring a list of any medications you take.

+How does payment work?

Payment is due at your visit, and we offer 0% financing for every credit type, so you can get treated today and pay over time. Questions before you come in? Call (516) 500-7400.
